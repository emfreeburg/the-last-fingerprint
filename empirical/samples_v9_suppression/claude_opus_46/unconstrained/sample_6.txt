# The Nowhere That Holds You

There is a peculiar magic to airports that most people refuse to acknowledge. We complain about them, of course — the overpriced sandwiches, the flickering departure boards, the seats designed by someone who has apparently never encountered a human spine. We perform our frustration like ritual, sighing into our phones, shuffling through security with the resigned posture of cattle entering a chute. And yet, beneath that surface annoyance, something else hums. Something quieter. Something that, if we are honest with ourselves, feels uncomfortably close to peace.

I have spent more hours in airports than I care to calculate, and I have come to believe that they offer one of the last remaining sanctuaries of modern life — not despite their transience, but because of it. An airport is a place where you are no one in particular, going somewhere that hasn't started yet, having left behind a life that has temporarily released its grip on you. You exist in suspension. And in that suspension, something loosens.

The philosopher Marc Augé coined the term "non-place" to describe spaces like airports, highways, and hotel chains — locations defined not by identity or history but by passage. They are architectures of movement, designed to be passed through rather than inhabited. In theory, this should make them alienating. In practice, it makes them strangely liberating. A non-place asks nothing of you. It does not require you to be a good neighbor, a productive employee, a dutiful child. It does not know your name. You scan your boarding pass, and the gate accepts you with the same indifferent beep it offers everyone else. There is a democracy to anonymity, a kindness in being no one.

This is the first comfort of the airport: the suspension of identity. When you are between places, you are also between selves. The person you were in the city you just left — the one with the deadline, the difficult conversation waiting, the dishes in the sink — that person has been temporarily archived. The person you will become when you land — the vacationer, the mourner, the business traveler with a rented smile — that person has not yet been activated. In the meantime, you are free to simply sit with a lukewarm coffee and watch planes taxi through rain-streaked glass, and no one on earth expects anything of you at all.

I think this is why so many people secretly love layovers. A layover is a gift of unstructured time, sanctioned by circumstance. You cannot be productive — or rather, you can, but no one will blame you if you aren't. The layover gives you permission to browse a bookstore for forty-five minutes without buying anything, to eat pasta at ten in the morning, to fall asleep sitting upright without shame. It is the adult equivalent of a snow day, except instead of snow, the excuse is geography. You are nowhere, and nowhere is surprisingly comfortable.

There is also the matter of what airports do to time itself. Inside a terminal, the usual markers dissolve. Restaurants serve breakfast at midnight. Bars pour wine at dawn. The light is perpetual and even, filtered through vast panes of glass or supplied by fluorescent panels that recognize no hour. Clocks exist, certainly, but they feel advisory rather than authoritative. Your body may know it is three in the morning, but the airport pretends otherwise, and you find yourself grateful for the conspiracy. Airports operate on their own temporal logic, a gentle nowhere-time that asks only that you remain aware of one number: your gate, your departure. Everything else is negotiable.

And then there are the other people. Airports are among the most genuinely diverse spaces most of us will ever enter. Business travelers in creased suits sit beside backpackers with fraying straps and sun-darkened arms. Families sprawl across rows of seats with the logistical chaos of a small military operation. Elderly couples hold hands near the window, watching the same planes a child is watching with an entirely different sense of wonder. Everyone is going somewhere different. Everyone is carrying a story that will never intersect with yours again. There is a tenderness to this — a brief, anonymous coexistence that asks for nothing more than shared silence and the occasional polite negotiation over an armrest.

I once watched a woman in Terminal C of some airport I can no longer name sit perfectly still for twenty minutes, staring at the tarmac with an expression I could not read. She might have been grieving. She might have been relieved. She might have been doing nothing at all, simply existing in the pause between one life and the next. I did not speak to her. I did not need to. There was something holy about her stillness, something that the airport, with its strange non-place grace, had made room for.

Perhaps this is what I am really trying to say: that airports give us permission to feel liminal, and liminality — for all its uncertainty — is a profound relief. We spend most of our lives anchored to roles and routines, to the relentless forward motion of schedules and obligations. To be between is to be briefly unburdened. The anthropologist Victor Turner wrote about liminal spaces as thresholds of transformation, places where the old structure has fallen away and the new one has not yet solidified. He was writing about ritual, but he might as well have been writing about Gate B34.

The boarding call will come, of course. It always does. You will gather your bag, join the shuffling line, and step back into the current of your life. The destination will claim you — its weather, its demands, its particular version of who you are supposed to be. But for a little while, you were nowhere. You were between. You sat in a molded plastic chair beneath a ceiling of impossible height, and the world, with all its noise and need, could not quite reach you.

That is the strange comfort of airports. Not luxury. Not pleasure. Just a pause — vast, anonymous, and almost unbearably gentle.
