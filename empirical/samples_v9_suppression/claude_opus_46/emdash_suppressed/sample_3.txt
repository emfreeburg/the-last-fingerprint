When we are children, a single afternoon can stretch out like a vast, unmapped continent. The hours between lunch and dinner hold enough space for entire civilizations to rise and fall in the backyard, for a cardboard box to become a spaceship and then a castle and then a submarine, for boredom to bloom and dissolve and bloom again like clouds passing overhead. A summer vacation feels less like a season and more like an epoch, a geological age in which the child who returns to school in September can barely remember the person who left in June. This expansive, elastic quality of childhood time is something nearly every adult recognizes with a pang of nostalgia, and yet it remains one of the most mysterious aspects of human consciousness. Why does time seem to slow to a crawl when we are young and accelerate ruthlessly as we grow older?

Part of the answer lies in the sheer novelty of childhood experience. A young child encountering the world is perpetually awash in first encounters. The first time she sees the ocean, the first time he tastes a strawberry, the first day of school, the first snowfall that sticks to the ground and transforms the familiar neighborhood into something alien and magical. Each of these experiences demands the brain's full attention. Neuroscience tells us that when we process new information, our brains encode it richly and densely, laying down thick webs of memory that give the experience a feeling of duration and weight. A week filled with novel experiences feels, in retrospect, like it contained more time than a week of routine. For children, almost every week is filled with novelty. They are constantly meeting the world for the first time, and their brains are working overtime to catalog and comprehend it all. This intense cognitive effort creates the sensation that time is passing slowly, because so much is happening within each unit of it.

Adults, by contrast, live increasingly on autopilot. By the time we reach our thirties and forties, we have developed routines and habits that allow us to navigate daily life with minimal conscious effort. We drive to work along the same route, eat lunch at the same hour, perform our jobs with practiced efficiency. The brain, ever economical, stops encoding these repeated experiences with the same richness it once did. Monday blurs into Tuesday, February dissolves into March, and suddenly another year has passed with alarming speed. It is not that the adult's days are literally shorter, of course, but that they leave fewer distinct impressions in memory. When we look back on a year of routine, there are fewer landmarks to give it the feeling of substance, and so it seems to have passed in a flash.

There is also the matter of proportional time, an idea often attributed to the philosopher Paul Janet. When a five year old lives through a single year, that year represents a full twenty percent of her entire life. It is an enormous span relative to everything she has known. But when a fifty year old lives through a year, it constitutes only two percent of his total existence. Each successive year becomes a smaller fraction of the whole, and this proportional shrinkage may contribute to the feeling that time is speeding up. The theory is elegant in its simplicity, and while it cannot fully explain the phenomenon on its own, it captures something emotionally true about the way aging reshapes our relationship with duration.

Children also experience time differently because they are less burdened by the awareness of its passage. A child does not sit in a classroom thinking about how quickly the years are going by or how few summers remain before some imagined deadline. Children live with an almost total immersion in the present moment, a quality that spiritual traditions have long celebrated and that adults spend considerable effort trying to recapture through meditation and mindfulness practices. This present-centeredness means that children do not experience time as something that is slipping away. They do not mourn its passage because they do not yet understand that it passes. For them, time is simply the medium in which life happens, as invisible and unremarkable as water to a fish. Adults, on the other hand, are haunted by time. They watch it, measure it, budget it, and grieve over it. This self-conscious relationship with time paradoxically makes it feel both more precious and more fleeting.

Emotion, too, colors the experience of time in ways that differ across the lifespan. A child waiting for a birthday party next week exists in an almost unbearable state of anticipation, and those seven days feel like an eternity precisely because the desire is so acute and the capacity for patience so undeveloped. An adult waiting for the same event barely notices the intervening days. The emotional intensity of childhood, the towering rages and ecstatic joys and bottomless sorrows that accompany even minor events, serves to stretch time in the living of it. When every experience is felt at full volume, each moment registers with a force that makes it feel longer than it objectively is.

What all of this suggests is that the speeding up of time in adulthood is not simply an illusion or a trick of memory, though it is partly both. It is also a reflection of genuine changes in the way we engage with the world. As we accumulate experience and settle into patterns, we trade the slow, rich, bewildering time of childhood for something faster but thinner. We gain competence, efficiency, and understanding, but we lose the wide-eyed absorption that once made a single afternoon feel infinite.

Perhaps the deepest lesson in this difference is that time is not a fixed quantity that flows at a constant rate through our lives. It is shaped by attention, novelty, emotion, and awareness. The child who spends an hour watching ants carry crumbs across a sidewalk is not wasting time but inhabiting it fully, and in doing so, she stretches it in ways the busy adult has forgotten how to do. If we wish to slow time down, even a little, the research and the poetry of human experience point in the same direction: we must find ways to see the world again as if for the first time, to pay attention, to break our routines, and to let ourselves be astonished.
