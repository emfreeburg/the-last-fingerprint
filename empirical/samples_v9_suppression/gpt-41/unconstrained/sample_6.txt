There is a strange and gentle comfort that can be found in airports, those sprawling hubs of human activity that bridge the worlds of arrival and departure. For many, the mention of airports conjures images of long security lines, uncomfortable chairs, overpriced coffee, and the stress of scheduled movement. Yet, for those who have spent time lingering in terminals—whether out of necessity or by choice—there exists a peculiar tranquility rooted in the very nature of being between places, of existing in a pocket of time that is neither here nor there.

Airports are unique spaces, designed for transience. They are nowhere and everywhere, a nexus that connects the familiar with the unknown. To be in an airport is to dwell in a state of suspension. The destination has not yet been reached; whatever was left behind is already receding into the past. The ordinary rhythms of daily life—commutes, chores, the stability of one’s own bed—are paused, replaced by the ceaseless hum of announcements, the rolling wheels of carry-on luggage, and the distant roar of departing planes.

It is this suspension, this in-between place, that provides a certain comfort. In airports, expectations are lowered. The vast terminal becomes a liminal space, allowing for anonymity and rootlessness. One can sit for hours, watching other travelers, reading books, sipping coffee, and indulging in the simple act of waiting without guilt. Time is measured not by the ticking past of the clock but by the time left until boarding. The loosening of obligations—a rare gift in our productivity-driven world—fosters a gentle acceptance: for now, there is nothing to do but to be.

This comfort is grounded in the airport’s function as a threshold, a crossing point between the life one knows and the life that waits elsewhere. It is a place of infinite possibility, where every traveler carries with them the invisible threads of stories—some happy, some sad, some burstingly optimistic. The woman tapping away at her laptop might be headed for a crucial business meeting that will change her career. The family wrangling tired children may be journeying toward a reunion after years apart. Airports become arenas for beginnings and endings, arrivals and departures, each glance at the roster of flights an invitation to imagine another life.

There is also comfort to be found in the collective nature of airport life. In no other place are so many different people—divergent by nationality, race, religion, purpose—gathered, united for a brief moment by the shared experience of going somewhere else. One is reminded of the vastness of the world, and the ways in which our individual journeys intersect. The temporal community of an airport lounge is forged by commonality: everyone, for now, is simply in transit. The feeling of being between places is thus emboldened by the knowledge that others, too, inhabit this space of transition, this collective pause.

Added to this is the subtle thrill of possibility that airports evoke. The sheer number of destinations, displayed in columns on glowing screens, hints at lives other than our own, sparks daydreams of impulsive escape. If the promise of a different life is ever real, it is in airports, where the boundary between one’s actual plans and infinite alternatives is as thin as a boarding pass. One can, for a time, step outside the strictures of identity: here, no one knows your backstory, your hopes, your failures. The only thing that matters is your destination, and the only people who know it are the strangers seated beside you.

Moreover, the rituals of airports—removing shoes at security, presenting passports, waiting for the announcement to board—offer a comforting predictability. These routines, learned by rote, provide structure and reassurance amid the uncertainty of travel. The monotony of the process, often maligned, can serve as a balm, imposing order on the chaos of modern life. In this way, airports function both as gateways and as sanctuaries, offering a reprieve from the outside world and its relentless demands.

Often, airports elicit a reflective state of mind. With nothing but time and no pressing obligations, one’s thoughts may wander to what lies ahead or what has just ended. The mind, like the body, is in transit. The airport offers a rare opportunity to process change, to compose oneself for a new chapter, or, occasionally, to grieve what was left behind. Some of the most important realizations, reconciliations, or retreats—from heartbreak, from routine, from the self—happen while gazing out at tarmacs lined with jets. The place itself is full of farewells and reunions, reminding every traveler of the impermanence that underpins life.

This feeling of in-betweenness—of liminality—has a certain poetry. Anthropologists refer to “liminal spaces” as transitional phases imbued with ambiguity, openness, and potential. Airports are the literal embodiment of this; within their walls, one is neither truly at home nor fully gone. It is a borderland, where the rules of the ordinary world are temporarily suspended, and anything, for a moment, feels possible.

In the end, the strange comfort of airports lies in their embrace of the transient, their insistence that not every moment require productivity or purpose beyond itself. To be in an airport is to surrender, briefly, to uncertainty and to acknowledge the gift of being unanchored. It offers respite from the relentless drive to arrive, to accomplish, to define oneself in certain terms. In airports, we are all souls in motion—bound by departure times, yet free in a way that is difficult to find elsewhere.

So next time you find yourself reclining in a too-bright departure lounge, or standing in line behind a family with too many suitcases, pause for a moment and feel the comfort that this nowhere place can offer. Allow yourself to drift, to be between places—not quite belonging, not quite escaping, but simply being—until the world calls you forward once more.
