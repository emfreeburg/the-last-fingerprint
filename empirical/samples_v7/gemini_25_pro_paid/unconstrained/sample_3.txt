## The Long Summer and the Fleeting Year: How Children and Adults Experience Time

Time, as physicists tell us, is a fundamental dimension of the universe, marching forward with the unyielding regularity of a metronome. For every human being, a minute is sixty seconds, and a year is a fixed orbit around the sun. Yet, this objective reality stands in stark contrast to our subjective experience. The same block of time that feels like an eternity to a child—a long, sun-drenched summer vacation—can feel like a fleeting moment to an adult, a year that vanishes in the blink of an eye. This profound divergence in temporal perception is not a mere quirk of memory but a fundamental difference in consciousness, rooted in a complex interplay of neurobiology, the psychology of novelty, and the structured architecture of our lives.

At its core, the difference begins in the brain. A child’s brain is a voracious learning machine, constantly forging new neural pathways as it encounters the world for the first time. Every sight, sound, and sensation is novel, requiring significant cognitive resources to process and encode. This dense processing of new information creates a richer, more detailed memory log. Neuroscientist David Eagleman suggests that when our brains encounter novel situations, they record memories in greater detail. When we look back on those periods, the density of the memories makes the time feel as though it lasted longer. For a child, almost everything is novel: the first day of school, the first taste of a lemon, the first time seeing snow. Their days are packed with unique, memorable data points, stretching their perception of time like a new elastic band.

In contrast, the adult brain is a master of efficiency.
