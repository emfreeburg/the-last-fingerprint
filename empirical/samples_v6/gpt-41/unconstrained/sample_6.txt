**The Strange Comfort of Airports: On the Solace of Being Between Places**

Between each point of departure and every destination, there exists a peculiar threshold—a space that belongs to neither home nor away, a liminal world suspended between past and future. Airports, with their gleaming corridors, their endless processions of travelers, and their strange blend of anonymity and routine, have become the modern person’s most vivid manifestation of being “in-between.” There is, for many, a unique comfort to be found here, one that springs not from belonging, but from the very absence of it. The airport dweller is adrift, unanchored yet quietly reassured by the flow of movement and the promise of passage.

Airports are paradoxical places. On the one hand, they are frenetic hubs of activity, every minute orchestrated on a tight schedule, every gate a confluence of urgency and impatience. Yet on the other hand, airports are pockets of waiting, of suspended animation. Here, time blurs. The clock ticks, but its meaning is offset by flight schedules and boarding calls, by inexplicable delays and the stretching elasticity of layovers. In this paradox lies the first hint of comfort: within airports, one is granted permission—if only by necessity—to simply exist. There is no pressure to do anything but wait.

This waiting is not the idle kind found on park benches or in lines at the grocery store. It is a purposeful waiting. There is a flight on the horizon, with a boarding pass in-hand—a future already arranged, that will happen with or without your active participation. In the interim, you are free from expectation. There are no chores to be done, no demands to be met, few decisions to make. Everything is prescribed: check in, pass security, find your gate, wait. Perhaps grab an overpriced cup of coffee, watch as fellow travelers stream past, and allow yourself to be swept along by the collective anticipation.

This surrender of agency can be remarkably soothing. For those often burdened by the grind of regular life, with its litany of responsibilities, the structure imposed by the airport is a surprising relief. Decisions are whittled to a minimum; identities are rendered temporarily irrelevant. No one knows you, and no one, for the most part, cares. In an age of hyperconnectivity, the sense of being untethered—even just for a few hours on hard plastic chairs—offers a rare and welcome anonymity.

Moreover, airports are sites where everyone is equally out of place. The business executive in a tailored suit, the exhausted parent juggling snacks and children, the backpacker with a dog-eared guidebook—all are strangers to the space, all are only passing through. This shared state of flux forges a subtle camaraderie. Small rituals abound: the polite shuffling in security queues, the furtive glances for available charging outlets, the collective groan at the announcement of a delay. These are the “rules” of the in-between, customs followed by a tribe that exists only in transit.

What deepens the comfort of airports is the sense of possibility they engender. Each terminal is a crossroads, each departure board a tapestry of far-flung places and names spoken with yearning or routine. Airports are in perpetual motion, and as their denizens, we become part of that restless movement. There is promise in every concourse: the possibility of reunion, of escape, of transformation. Old selves can be sloughed off like the security checkpoint’s trays of emptied pockets; new selves can be tried on with the ease of slipping through duty-free aisles. A person walking through an airport is, in a very real sense, between versions of themselves.

This liminality is not without its emotional resonance. The poet John O’Donohue once wrote, “At any given time you are neither here nor there.” Airports make literal this metaphorical state. They allow, even force us, to register the transitions in our lives—endings and beginnings brushed tightly together. Partings and reunions play out in open view: every tearful goodbye at security has its mirror image in an ecstatic welcome at arrivals. Sitting at your gate, you become a witness to these human dramas, a participant in the theater of passage.

For many, this is also a space of reflection. Disconnected from the routines of daily life, freed from distraction by the very lack of belonging, you may find yourself thinking differently—about where you’ve come from, where you’re going, and what both mean. Airports are conducive to introspection, their hum of white noise making way for thoughts that are easily muffled elsewhere. The view through expansive windows, of planes trundling down runways and climbing into the sky, is a reminder of both our insignificance and our potential.

Of course, not all experience airports as comforting. Delays, cancellations, and crowds can fray tempers; the impersonal efficiency can feel dehumanizing; the prices can be laughably steep. Yet even within the frustrations lie kernels of the same comfort: the reassurance that, for now, your course is set. There is nothing to do but wait, to be present in the transition.

In an increasingly rootless world, airports provide a template for existence in flux. We are all, to varying degrees, between places: leaving behind what was, not yet arrived at what will be. The strange comfort of airports lies in this reflection of our broader human experience. They are spaces where we can simply be—unrushed, unnoticed, but undeniably in motion. In those hours perched at a gate, cradled by tinny announcements and the lull of jet engines, we may find the solace of being unmoored, the freedom that comes not from arrival, but from the passage itself.
