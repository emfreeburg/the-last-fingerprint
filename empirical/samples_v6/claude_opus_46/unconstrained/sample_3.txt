# The Long Afternoon: How Children and Adults Experience Time Differently

There is a peculiar phenomenon that nearly every adult recognizes but struggles to explain. The summers of childhood seemed to stretch on forever—endless, luminous expanses of days that each contained a small universe of experience. Now, entire years collapse into what feels like a matter of weeks. December arrives with a jarring swiftness, and we find ourselves asking the same bewildered question: where did the time go? The difference between how children and adults experience time is not merely a matter of nostalgia or selective memory. It is a profound divergence rooted in neuroscience, psychology, and the fundamental ways we engage with the world around us.

To understand why time feels so different at age eight than it does at age forty, we must first consider the role of novelty. A child's world is saturated with first experiences. The first day of school, the first time swimming in the ocean, the first thunderstorm watched from a window—each of these events demands intense cognitive processing. The brain must build new neural pathways, categorize unfamiliar information, and encode rich, detailed memories. This process of deep encoding creates what psychologists call a "temporal illusion." When we look back on a period filled with vivid, distinct memories, our brain interprets that richness as evidence of a long duration. In other words, more memories equal a subjective sense of more time having passed.

Adults, by contrast, live in a world that has become largely familiar. The drive to work, the weekly grocery run, the cycle of meetings and obligations—these experiences are processed with minimal cognitive effort. The brain, ever efficient, does not bother to encode routine events in elaborate detail. Days blur together. Weeks become indistinguishable from one another. When an adult tries to look back on the past six months, they may find alarmingly few distinct memories to anchor their recollection, and the period seems to have evaporated. The German psychologist William Friedman described this as the difference between "experienced time" and "remembered time," and it is in remembered time that the disparity between children and adults becomes most dramatic.

There is also a mathematical elegance to the phenomenon, first proposed by the philosopher Paul Janet in the nineteenth century. Janet suggested that we perceive time in proportion to the total amount of time we have already lived. For a five-year-old, one year represents twenty percent of their entire existence—a vast, weighty fraction. For a fifty-year-old, that same year is a mere two percent, a sliver so thin it barely registers. This proportional theory helps explain the relentless acceleration that adults feel as they age. Each successive year becomes a smaller fraction of the whole, and the subjective experience of its passage shrinks accordingly. The theory is elegantly simple, though it does not account for everything. After all, a year spent traveling the world at age fifty can feel far longer and richer than a monotonous year spent at age twenty-five.

Beyond memory and proportion, there is the matter of attention. Children possess a quality of presence that adults have largely lost. Watch a child crouched over an anthill, observing the insects with total absorption, and you are witnessing a consciousness fully immersed in the present moment. Children are not yet burdened with the mental habits that fragment adult attention—the endless planning, worrying, and ruminating that pull the mind away from immediate experience. When we are truly present, time seems to slow down. Each moment is felt and inhabited. Adults, on the other hand, spend a remarkable amount of their waking lives on autopilot, their minds wandering to future tasks or past regrets. Studies in cognitive psychology suggest that the average adult spends nearly half their waking hours thinking about something other than what they are currently doing. This habitual mental absence causes time to slip past unnoticed, like a river flowing beneath a bridge we never bother to look over.

The emotional texture of time also differs between children and adults. For children, emotions are immediate, overwhelming, and deeply felt. A single afternoon can contain the soaring joy of a game with friends and the devastating sorrow of a scraped knee, each emotion experienced with full intensity. This emotional vividness contributes to the sense that time is expansive and full. Adults have generally learned to regulate and mute their emotional responses—a necessary adaptation for functioning in society, but one that flattens the emotional landscape of daily life. When every day feels more or less the same emotionally, the days lose their distinctiveness and begin to merge.

There are also neurological factors at play. Research suggests that the brain's internal clock—regulated by dopamine levels and the function of the basal ganglia—operates differently at various stages of development. Children's brains are still maturing, and their neurochemistry may literally process temporal information at a different rate. Some researchers have proposed that the developing brain takes in more "frames per second" of conscious experience, much like a high-speed camera, which would make each objective minute contain more subjective experience for a child than for an adult.

The implications of this divergence extend beyond mere curiosity. Understanding why time accelerates can motivate us to resist its compression. If novelty, attention, and emotional engagement are the ingredients that slow time down, then adults have a blueprint for reclaiming some of that childhood expansiveness. Seeking new experiences, practicing mindfulness, breaking routines, traveling to unfamiliar places, learning new skills—these are not just pleasurable activities. They are, in a very real sense, ways of creating more time, or at least more of the subjective experience of it.

Perhaps the most poignant aspect of this difference is that it is invisible to those experiencing it. Children do not know that their summers are long; they have no basis for comparison. And adults often do not realize how compressed their experience has become until they pause to reflect and find years missing from their felt sense of life. The awareness itself, however, can be a gift—an invitation to live more deliberately, to pay attention, and to remember that the richness of a life is measured not in the years that pass, but in the moments we are truly present to receive.
