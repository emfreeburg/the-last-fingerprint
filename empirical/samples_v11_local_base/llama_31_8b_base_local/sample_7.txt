In my early teens I took classical guitar lessons. My teacher was an affable man in his late thirties with long blond hair and a scruffy beard who wore loose black T-shirts that he claimed were given away by a local rock band. He lived above his studio, where his girlfriend ran a small import business.

I was a fairly good student for him—better than most of the other kids who came in for lessons—and I picked up the basics easily. My fingers found their way across the fretboard without much trouble and I learned to read music quickly enough. But as far as practicing went, I wasn’t very motivated. When my teacher asked me what kind of pieces I wanted to work on, I told him I didn’t care too much about classical guitar and was more interested in rock. He suggested we start with some Beatles songs.

I practiced a little here and there, but I didn’t really feel any progress or satisfaction from it. If anything, the time I put into playing music seemed like an obligation to do something I didn’t necessarily enjoy doing. My friends were all into sports, so if there was an activity that we could engage in together after school and on weekends, it was going out for a game of ball hockey with sticks made from broom handles. Playing the guitar just wasn’t fun anymore.

I went through a period when I would get home from school, sit down at my instrument, try to play something, then immediately put it aside and run off to do something more interesting with my friends. My teacher was patient with me and tried different approaches to motivate me. He invited me over for dinner once and talked to me about how he’d taken lessons when he was younger and how hard work had paid off, but nothing seemed to change.

One day, after I had been playing the guitar for several months, my teacher asked if there were any songs that I really wanted to learn but hadn’t found the time to. Without thinking, I blurted out: “I’ve always wanted to play ‘Stairway to Heaven.’” He stared at me in disbelief.

“Don’t you have better things to spend your time on?” he asked. I nodded and tried to shrug it off. He didn’t press the matter any further.

When my teacher invited me over for dinner again, this time his girlfriend came with him. She was a strikingly beautiful woman who had a lot of energy and always seemed excited about something. At one point during the meal, she told everyone at the table to close their eyes while she put on her favorite record player. Then she asked each person to open their eyes and describe what they thought it sounded like.

When it was my turn, I could tell that I wasn’t very good at this game because no one else got even close to guessing correctly.

“You’re listening too hard,” she said when I finished speaking. “Think about what you actually hear instead of trying so hard to figure out where it comes from.”

I took her advice and listened more carefully to the sounds around me. It made a big difference in my life. From then on, I spent most of my free time listening to music. If I wasn’t doing that, I was playing ball hockey with friends or going for walks alone in nature.

One day during a walk through the woods near my home, something clicked inside my head. I realized that if I listened closely enough, I could make out individual notes coming from trees, birds, insects, and other living things.

I remember thinking: “Maybe this is how animals communicate with each other.” And then it hit me: “This is how music was created in the first place!”

In my early teens, while learning to play a musical instrument, I realized that listening carefully and deeply helps us understand ourselves better and connect with others more easily.
