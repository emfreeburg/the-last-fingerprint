There’s something special about old things. Not so much antiques as old things that have been loved and used over time.

In my house there are old tools from my father and grandfather that I cherish and use with care. They were expensive when new, but now they’ve become heirlooms. Old things have stories, and it’s a privilege to know the story of something that has been around for a while. It’s even more special if you’re able to preserve and add to its history.

I have an old Stanley No 80 2″ x 12″ Jointer Planer with a very interesting history. It is one of the rarest pieces from the early 20th century, having been designed in 1916 by James E. Sargent who then went on to found his own tool company. This particular planer was bought new and used for years at my grandfather’s lumber mill. He kept it until he died in 1994, when I inherited it along with a variety of other tools.

The machine is an amazing piece of engineering that combines power and simplicity into one beautiful device. It has no belts or chains to adjust; the belt system was designed by Sargent himself so that everything was contained within the frame. There’s also not much exposed metal on this jointer/planer, which makes it safe for kids (and cats) to play around with.

When I first got it there were some minor issues but nothing major like a broken blade or stripped gears – just something small like missing knobs and handles from over 100 years of use. Over time though I found myself using this machine more frequently than my newer ones because it felt better made and easier to work with. After so many years the quality of materials used back then really shows through.

As I continued working on it, I noticed some rust in certain places where oil would accumulate over long periods of use due to lack of maintenance by previous owners. So I decided to give my trusty old friend a good cleaning up with WD-40 and some elbow grease before putting it back together again like new!

I’m happy that I got my hands on this piece because now when someone asks about where they can find one for themselves – I know exactly what kind of features to point out which will make their experience much better than if they just bought any other generic model available today at local home improvement stores.

In conclusion, owning something like this brings joy not only in terms of its functionality but also because you get to see how something was made before mass production techniques were invented and you can even feel proud knowing that your family has been using it since then too!

• #### An Interesting Paper on the History of the Calculus

A friend who knows a thing or two about calculus sent me a link to an interesting paper titled The Historical Roots of the Modern Concept of Fluxions by Carl Benjamin Boyer. It was published in the Isis, Vol 47, No. 2 (Jun., 1956), pp. 181-192.

If you are interested at all in the history of mathematics, I highly recommend reading this. A few years ago I took a graduate-level course on the History of Mathematics and it was by far my favorite class ever.

The paper opens with:

During most of the seventeenth century, Newton called his calculus "the method of fluxions" or simply "fluxions." The word "fluxion," however, had been used by earlier mathematicians before Newton to designate what we now call an infinitesimal variation in magnitude. For example, Cavalieri (1598-1647) referred to the limit concept as "fluxions and fluents"; Leibniz spoke of a quantity which is "in flux" or in "motion," and of its "variation"; Barrow applied this term to the derivative with respect to time; while Gregory called his calculus of infinitesimals "the method of motion." The most comprehensive use of the word seems to have been that by Fermat (1601-1665) who employed it in connection with all limiting processes in mathematics. In a letter dated 25 February 1639, he used the words "fluxion" and "in flux" when discussing his concept of an infinitesimal increase or decrease in the length of a curve; on several occasions he applied these terms to the concept of limit as it appears in his method of exhaustion for rectification and quadrature problems.

• #### A Few Notes on the History of Mathematics

A few years ago I took a graduate-level course titled, The History of Mathematics. It was by far my favorite class ever. One thing that struck me about this class is how much the history of math has to do with wars and politics. This article is one example: Euler's Greatest Mistake.

There are some things worth noting from this article:

In 1732, Euler published his famous Introductio in Analysin Infinitorum. It was a groundbreaking text on analysis which introduced many fundamental ideas and concepts of calculus, including limits and power series. The book also contained the first proof that the sum of the squares of the tangents to an equilateral triangle at its vertices is equal to the square of the incircle.

The Introductio was translated into English in 1822 by John Colson (1680-1760), who was Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge University for more than forty years. In his translation, Colson made a mistake when he stated that Euler’s equation had an incorrect sign.

After Euler died, the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss corrected Colson’s error and proved that Euler’s equation is correct by considering the geometry of complex numbers and their polar representation.

I find it interesting that a simple typo could lead to such a profound mathematical misunderstanding. It’s also funny how many historical events have been forgotten over time due to various factors like wars or other political changes.

• #### A Few More Examples of "WTF Is This?" Math

I came across a few more examples of what I call “WTF is this?!” math problems. These are problems that look weird or just don’t make sense because the answers don’t make sense, and they’re not easy to solve either.

The first one is from the book Mathematics for High School Students by G. H. Hardy:

Solve the following equation: $x^2 + 5 = 0.$

You can try any method you like, but I’m sure it will take some time before you get a solution! This problem appears in Chapter VII of the book “Mathematics for High School Students” by G.H. Hardy and E.J. Milne.

I don’t think anyone has ever been able to find an exact solution for this equation, so if we wanted to solve this problem ourselves we would have no choice but to use trial-and-error methods (i.e., guessing). The most common way people try solving it is by substituting numbers in place of x until they get something that looks close enough as the answer. For example:

$0^2 + 5 = -5$

which seems pretty close considering we only added two more terms compared to our original equation $x^2 + 5.$
