# The Strange Comfort of Airports

Airports occupy a peculiar space in human experience, existing neither wholly in the world of departure nor arrival, yet somehow belonging to both and neither simultaneously. They are sanctuaries for the in-between, places where the normal rules of society seem to suspend themselves in favor of a temporary, liminal existence. There is something profoundly comforting about this strange suspension, this moment when you have left behind the obligations and routines of one place but have not yet arrived at the demands of another. In an increasingly chaotic world, airports offer a paradoxical refuge, a space where the very act of transition becomes its own destination, and where being nowhere becomes a kind of peace.

The moment you enter an airport terminal, you step across an invisible threshold. Behind you lies the everyday world with its expectations, responsibilities, and anchoring relationships. The person who dropped you off at the curb exists in one realm, and you in another. This separation feels almost ritualistic, as though you are shedding one identity temporarily and preparing to don another. There is liberation in this liminal space, a freedom that comes from the knowledge that for the next few hours, you are untethered from the normal expectations of productivity and social engagement. The rules that govern ordinary life seem to loosen their grip here. You are permitted to sit alone for hours without judgment. You are allowed to do nothing, to simply exist and watch, to wander without purpose or destination.

This permission to exist without agenda is increasingly rare in contemporary life. In airports, there is an understanding, implicit and widely shared, that transition requires a kind of temporal suspension. You are neither at work nor at home. You cannot meaningfully conduct business in most cases, as your attention is already half-turned toward departure. You cannot truly settle into leisure because your mind knows this location is temporary. This in-between status creates a unique psychological space where the ordinary anxieties that drive us forward lose some of their force. You cannot be productive in an airport in the traditional sense, so you are excused from trying. This excusal feels like a gift.

Walking through airport corridors, observing other travelers, there is a sense of collective understanding. Everyone here is in transition. Everyone is between. This creates an odd form of community, not built on shared interests or histories, but on shared temporal experience. The businessman in the suit checking his watch repeatedly, the young couple with interlocked fingers heading toward an adventure, the elderly woman carefully counting her carry-on items, the student sleeping with their head against a window, the family trying to keep children entertained while time passes. All are suspended together in this space outside normal life. There is something deeply human about this gathering of strangers united by nothing but the fact that they are all briefly nowhere.

The architecture of airports seems almost designed to encourage this contemplative suspension. The vast open spaces, the high ceilings that seem to stretch toward infinity, the diffuse lighting that never quite matches the time of day outside, all combine to create an environment that feels slightly removed from reality. Airports are often criticized for their sterility, their lack of character, their interchangeable sameness across the globe. But perhaps this very quality contributes to their comfort. There is something soothing about this lack of specificity, this intentional refusal to anchor you to any particular place or time. An airport in Singapore and an airport in London operate by similar logics, with similar shops and similar sitting areas and similar announcement systems. This uniformity, rather than being alienating, can feel oddly reassuring. You need not figure out how to exist here; the space itself tells you how to be.

Time functions differently in airports as well. Watch in hand or eyes on phone screen, travelers are acutely aware of time, yet time itself seems to move strangely. An hour waiting for a flight feels different from an hour spent in an office or at home. It seems at once to compress and expand, to move both too slowly and too quickly. You become hyperaware of minutes, yet somehow less anxious about them passing. Perhaps this is because in an airport, you have already surrendered agency over time. The flight will leave when it leaves. Your arrival time is fixed. There is nothing you can do to alter these facts, and this surrender to temporal inevitability can be curiously freeing.

The stranger you become in an airport is a different person than you are elsewhere. We all perform versions of ourselves in different contexts, but the airport performance is unique. Here, you are no one's colleague, no one's friend, no one's family member. You are anonymously yourself, observed by no one who knows your history or expectations. This anonymity, paired with the knowledge that you will never see most of these people again, creates a kind of freedom. You could sit and read an obscure book without anyone wondering why. You could eat an unusual combination of foods for lunch. You could sit and stare into space for hours, and it would seem perfectly normal. Everyone in an airport is performing a kind of solo existence, and everyone understands and accepts this.

There is also comfort in airports' radical functionalism. Everything in the space is there for a reason related to travel, to movement, to transition. There is a beautiful honesty in this. The airport does not pretend to be anything other than what it is. It is not trying to be a home or a workplace or a social venue. It is simply and straightforwardly a place of passage. This clarity of purpose can be deeply soothing. You know exactly why you are here and what is expected of you. The airport has no hidden agendas, no social subtexts requiring decoding. It is refreshingly straightforward in a world often characterized by mixed messages and unclear expectations.

Ultimately, the comfort of airports lies in their permission for a kind of peace available almost nowhere else. They permit you to be still without being idle, to be alone without being lonely, to be nowhere without feeling lost. They offer a rare moment in modern life when forward progress is someone else's responsibility, when you have already left the past and have not yet arrived at the future. In this suspended moment, you are free to simply be. And in a world that constantly demands becoming, airports offer the strange and precious gift of being allowed to simply be, if only for a few hours, in the peaceful nowhere between here and there.
